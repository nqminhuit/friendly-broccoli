## Opening — The Comfort of a Familiar Detective

![](holmes-and-then-there-were-none-01.png)

There is a particular comfort in watching Sherlock Holmes enter a room. Whatever has happened, someone is going to look carefully. The confused account will meet a patient question. Something everybody else walked past may finally receive attention.

Agatha Christie's And Then There Were None takes that comfort away. There is no familiar detective standing safely outside the danger, ready to explain it afterwards.

So let us give the guests an unexpected companion.

Imagine Arthur Conan Doyle's Holmes arriving on Soldier Island with all ten original visitors. Watson is not with him. He has not read the novel, and he knows none of its secrets. We are borrowing him at the height of his powers, not trying to reconcile two fictional calendars.

Would his presence restore that comforting promise? Or has Christie built a situation in which even the right questions might come too late?

## 1. Before the First Death — An Invitation to Question

![](holmes-and-then-there-were-none-02.png)

Holmes would have something to investigate before there was a body.

The invitations offer different reasons for coming. Some visitors expect employment; others believe they are meeting acquaintances. The hosts are absent. Who has actually met them? Who can verify the arrangements, rather than repeat what a letter says?

I imagine Holmes asking to compare those letters. Not because handwriting would instantly identify a murderer, but because the discrepancies might reveal how carefully each visitor had been approached.

He could notice a coordinated deception without yet understanding its purpose.

There is a complication, though. Our extra visitor has changed the conditions. The person who arranged everything might abandon the plan rather than face him. To explore the mystery, let us suppose the evening begins despite his unexpected arrival. That is a premise we grant the thought experiment, not something Holmes has achieved.

And his reputation would not make him safe from suspicion. When the recorded accusations name the original company but leave him out, the others might wonder whether this celebrated investigator is directing the performance.

Before he can challenge anyone else's account, Holmes may have to give a convincing account of himself. That seems a fair price for entering somebody else's mystery.

## 2. Evidence Versus Performance

![](holmes-and-then-there-were-none-03.png)

Anthony Marston's sudden death turns the questions urgent. Holmes might recognize poisoning, but recognizing danger is not the same as being able to reverse it.

His useful next step would be less dramatic: preserve what can still be examined.

What happened to the glass? Who handled the drinks? Which movements were actually witnessed? He would want separate accounts before repeated conversation made everybody remember the same tidy sequence.

There is something very Holmesian about protecting an untidy fact from a polished explanation.

I can picture him retracing a route through the house, checking what could actually be seen from a doorway. A convincing account of someone's movements might become less convincing when measured against the rooms.

But we should resist furnishing him with convenient clues Christie never supplied. We cannot invent a distinctive footprint beside the poison, then congratulate him for finding it. His chemical knowledge does not produce a laboratory on an isolated island. A suspicion still needs a test he can perform.

The distinction I would keep returning to is between what someone observed and what someone concluded. A person may have seen a door close without seeing who passed through it. An estimated time can harden into an exact one through repetition.

Christie is interested in that gap too. This is not a contest between Holmes's facts and Christie's feelings. Both writers understand that evidence reaches us through people, and people can be mistaken, frightened, or deliberately helpful in the wrong direction.

## 3. Guilt Is Not Proof

![](holmes-and-then-there-were-none-04.png)

The guests give Holmes plenty of reasons to distrust them. The difficulty would be deciding what each reason means.

Vera Claythorne's distress might draw his attention. It would not tell him, by itself, that she deliberately encouraged a child into a fatal swim. Readers enter memories that an investigator cannot inspect.

Philip Lombard presents a different trap. He admits abandoning men to die in Africa, with an appalling racial contempt. That frankness is not a certificate of honesty about everything else. Nor does it prove he is poisoning his companions.

William Blore has professional experience and a false identity. Emily Brent has a hard conviction that her treatment of her pregnant servant was justified, despite the young woman's suicide. Their evasions and certainties would need different questions.

I would want Holmes to distinguish a concealed past from concealed movements that evening. The first may explain a lie without explaining the present murder.

His manner could make that difficult. A devastating deduction is entertaining when we watch it from an armchair. Being publicly exposed by a stranger might make a frightened person less willing to speak again.

Holmes can be tactful, and he understands motives as well as material traces. He would need those abilities here. If he treated every guest as an interesting specimen, he might collect impressive observations while losing the cooperation that could make them useful.

## 4. Breaking the Rhyme

![](holmes-and-then-there-were-none-05.png)

Once the deaths begin to follow the nursery rhyme, the island seems to acquire a timetable nobody can escape.

Holmes would not need to accept that timetable. A rhyme cannot kill anyone. Someone still needs access, time, and an opportunity.

Yet it would be unfair to say the original guests never think of precautions. They search the island. They consider weapons and medicines. The novel's trap is more interesting than a roomful of people forgetting to look for danger.

Holmes's contribution might be checking who controls each precaution. Locking something away is not enough if the arrangement places unquestioned trust in a possible suspect. He should submit his own possessions and movements to the same scrutiny.

I can imagine him pressing for witnessed handling of medicines, shared records, and fewer occasions when someone goes off alone. General Macarthur should not simply be left by the shore. If Rogers needs firewood, someone can go with him.

But we must let these measures change the story. We cannot insist that every original murder still happens in its appointed order, merely so Holmes can explain it later.

Nor can we promise he prevents the earliest deaths. In the novel, Ethel Rogers receives poison in her brandy before the pattern becomes clear. If Holmes has not intervened before then, his presence alone supplies neither an antidote nor advance warning of what she has taken.

Escape and signals to the mainland should matter more than completing the investigation. The cleverest response to a deadly puzzle may be refusing to stay inside it.

## 5. The Death That Needs Checking

![](holmes-and-then-there-were-none-06.png)

Here is where I think Holmes would have his strongest opportunity, if events reached the corresponding point: the apparent death of Justice Wargrave.

In Christie's solution, Edward Armstrong helps the judge stage his death, believing the deception will help expose the real murderer. The judge's standing helps make that proposal respectable. Armstrong does not merely mistake an ingenious disguise for a corpse. He knowingly gives the others a false medical account.

Wargrave then kills the man who could reveal the arrangement.

The deception removes the judge from the surviving guests' calculations. They keep asking which living person could be responsible, while treating the list of the dead as settled.

Holmes might interrupt that reasoning. Did he examine the supposed wound himself? What did he personally establish, and what rests entirely on Armstrong's statement?

That would not require contempt for doctors. Expert testimony is often essential. It would require recognizing that the expert is also a participant in this particular case.

If Holmes insisted on checking, the trick might collapse. If Armstrong disclosed the proposal beforehand, Holmes might stop it being attempted. Neither possibility proves that he could reconstruct every earlier killing at once.

And Wargrave need not obligingly continue. He could resist inspection, alter his scheme, or turn against the detective. If Holmes accepted Armstrong's account, the deception could succeed again.

What makes the encounter interesting is the similarity between the two men's methods. Each understands staging, attention, and the authority of a convincing account. Holmes would be investigating someone who has arranged not just the evidence, but the way the company discusses it.

The crucial observation might therefore be surprisingly modest: we have stopped questioning this because someone told us it was settled.

## 6. The Verdict — Solving, Proving, Saving

![](holmes-and-then-there-were-none-07.png)

My answer is yes: Holmes has a credible route to exposing Wargrave. I would not call it an inevitable victory.

He could suspect the judge, discover the staged death, and disrupt the plan. Building a case that accounts for the poisonings and later attacks would still require evidence. Preventing further harm would require practical help, not just an accusation.

Doyle himself allows that distinction. In The Five Orange Pips, Holmes's client is killed after seeking his help. The detective's intelligence does not make the interval before an explanation safe.

Christie's ending makes the cost still harsher. Vera shoots Lombard, then hangs herself. Wargrave then kills himself, making the scene agree with the others' earlier accounts of his supposed murder. His confession eventually supplies the explanation. The novel leaves no surviving island guest for the truth to rescue.

We cannot give Holmes that confession in advance. Nor should we expect him to reconstruct every detail simply because we know how the trick works.

There is also a moral challenge. Wargrave joins his desire to kill to a claim that he is punishing people the law cannot reach. Holmes has sometimes exercised private judgement himself. He would not arrive as a spotless representative of legal procedure.

But understanding the guests' wrongdoing does not oblige him to accept their execution. His most important refusal would be to let Wargrave turn their guilt into permission for another murder.

[Pause:2s]

The solution could begin with a discovery. A better ending would depend on what Holmes and the others did with it.

## Closing — Before the Explanation

![](closing_6.png)

Thank you for joining me in this imagined meeting between two very different kinds of detective story.

I would give Holmes a real chance on Soldier Island. His greatest advantage might not be an extraordinary observation, but the habit of returning to an ordinary question: how do we know that?

Still, I would not want him to reduce Christie's guests to pieces he moves more successfully than Wargrave. They would have to trust him, contradict him, and sometimes help him see what he had missed. Even without Watson, he could not do everything alone.

Which moment do you think would change the story: comparing the invitations, preserving the first evidence, or refusing to accept the judge's apparent death? Please mark spoilers if you share your thoughts.

For me, the most moving possibility is not Holmes standing over the finished puzzle. It is someone who died in the original story still having time to answer his next question. An explanation cannot return a life. Arriving at one a little sooner might save it.
