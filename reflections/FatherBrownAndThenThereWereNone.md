## Opening — A Priest on Soldier Island

![](father-brown-and-then-there-were-none-01.png)

Imagine bringing Father Brown into a room where everybody has something to hide. He would hardly command the room. A small, ordinary priest, listening while more confident people explain themselves: at first, you might wonder what difference he could possibly make.

That is what interests me about placing him inside And Then There Were None by Agatha Christie. Not whether he could announce the answer before dinner, but what he would hear in conversations that fear makes almost impossible.

G. K. Chesterton gave his detective an unusual qualification. Brown understands wrongdoing without believing that wrongdoers belong to some separate kind of humanity. On Christie's island, that belief would be tested terribly.

## Understanding Evil from Within

![](father-brown-and-then-there-were-none-02.png)

For our invented premise, suppose Brown visits the island's servants, misses the departing boat, and finds that worsening weather prevents his return. All ten original characters remain. He is an additional visitor, not a replacement victim with an invented secret crime.

His presence would already disturb the murderer's arrangement. Wargrave might delay, change his methods, or abandon the attempt. Let us explore what Brown could bring if the danger nevertheless began, rather than insist that every death must happen on schedule.

In The Secret of Father Brown, the priest explains his approach with a startling claim: he has committed the murders himself. Not physically. He means that he has imagined himself committing them, searching his own mind for the desires that could make such actions possible.

That is a demanding kind of self-examination. Think of something as ordinary as wanting to punish somebody who has humiliated you. Brown follows such feelings far enough to understand how they might become something dreadful.

He does not conclude that everyone is equally guilty. He refuses to make understanding depend on thinking himself morally superior.

This gives our reflection its central contrast. Brown looks inward to recognize a temptation he must resist. Wargrave constructs an elaborate argument for allowing himself to surrender to one.

## Different Faces of Guilt

![](father-brown-and-then-there-were-none-03.png)

The recorded accusations would confront Brown with more than a collection of concealed facts. He would hear different ways of living with responsibility.

Emily Brent believes she acted rightly in dismissing her pregnant servant, despite the young woman's subsequent suicide. Her religious language would not reassure Brown merely because he is religious too. He might recognize how easily a moral rule becomes a way of avoiding another person's need.

Vera Claythorne carries a different burden. We readers eventually learn how deliberately she encouraged a child towards a fatal swim. Brown would initially have only her words and behaviour. A moment of distress would give him reason for care and questions, not instant knowledge of her past.

General Macarthur's resignation would trouble him differently again. The general has sent his wife's lover towards death; now his own future seems to have lost its claim on him. Brown might hear danger in that surrender before hearing anything useful to an investigation.

I would not turn these conversations into a competition over who feels worst. Visible anguish does not prove honesty, and an absence of tears does not identify the island's killer. Brown would need to listen without confusing sympathy with acquittal.

## The Man Who Appoints Himself God

![](father-brown-and-then-there-were-none-04.png)

Justice Wargrave presents the sharpest challenge because his crime wears the appearance of moral purpose. His final confession describes both a hatred of wrongdoing and a longstanding desire to kill. He has found a way to make one serve the other.

The people he selects have escaped legal punishment. But that does not make his private investigation infallible, his ranking of their guilt trustworthy, or his pleasure in killing them disappear.

Here The Hammer of God becomes a striking companion to Christie's novel. Wilfred Bohun kills his brother with a small hammer dropped from a church tower. Brown solves a physical puzzle involving height and force, but he also understands the temptation attached to that height.

From above, people have begun to look small enough to dispose of. Wilfred imagines himself entitled to strike down a sinner. His devotion has become entangled with the fantasy of standing where only God should stand.

Wargrave builds his own version of that tower on Soldier Island. He decides whose life is forfeit, how long each person should suffer, and what shape the whole performance should take.

Brown would have reason to question the authority being claimed here. Yet Emily's certainty reminds us that self-righteousness is not a fingerprint. Recognizing Wargrave's kind of temptation could guide an inquiry; it could not finish one.

Christie's recurring rhyme gives the novel a tightening rhythm: another death, another missing figure. Brown's habit of lingering over a person would pull against that terrible counting. His presence would change the story's pace as well as its possibilities.

The deepest opposition would be between two ways of regarding flawed people. Wargrave arranges them into a pattern. Brown would keep finding reasons to speak with them individually.

That difference matters. A person becomes easier to kill when a label seems to explain everything about them.

## Suspicion Is Not Proof

![](father-brown-and-then-there-were-none-05.png)

Would this insight actually expose the murderer? Only if Brown could connect it with things that happened.

Chesterton does not give him psychology instead of observation. In The Blue Cross, Brown takes practical steps to protect the cross and leave a trail the police can follow. In The Queer Feet, he notices a change of walking pace that reveals a man moving between the roles of servant and gentleman. Human expectations matter because someone exploits them through ordinary physical actions.

On the island, the crucial deception involves Doctor Armstrong. Wargrave persuades him to help stage the judge's death, supposedly as a means of uncovering the murderer. The doctor knowingly gives the others a false medical assurance. Later, Wargrave kills him.

Brown might approach Armstrong through the discomfort of that dishonest promise. Has someone persuaded him that deceiving frightened people is his duty? If Armstrong disclosed the arrangement in an ordinary conversation, Brown would have a crucial witness account to investigate.

But Armstrong might remain silent. Brown is not a doctor, and questioning authority does not grant him the skill to certify a death. If he accepted the false account, he could be deceived too.

Nor could he borrow information from the final confession. The accusation against Wargrave concerns Edward Seton, a man he condemned to death. But Seton really was guilty in the novel. We learn that; Brown would need evidence to establish it. His suspicions must remain separate from our knowledge of the ending.

## Mercy Against the Murderer's Design

![](father-brown-and-then-there-were-none-06.png)

Meanwhile, his care would have to begin before his explanation was complete.

Brown would have good reason to resist the island's growing habit of leaving people alone with their fear. Staying near someone in distress, seeking help from the mainland, and protecting food and medicines would matter more urgently than producing an elegant theory. He might still miss a poisoning. A priest's presence is not an antidote.

I can picture him drawing a chair nearer to Vera, leaving the door open, and staying when conversation falters. No grand speech, just somebody unwilling to let her disappear into her fear. He need not treat her distress as either proof of innocence or a sentence already passed. Admitting responsibility without being reduced entirely to it might become possible—and weaken the despair on which Wargrave's final arrangement depends.

That would not erase the child she caused to drown. Mercy that forgets the victim would be another form of evasion. It would mean that Vera still has obligations to the living, rather than a duty to complete the murderer's ending.

There is a precedent in The Hammer of God: Brown prevents Wilfred's suicide, then leaves him to choose his next step. Wilfred gives himself up. That is a risky act of trust, not a guarantee that every killer will respond. We should not borrow Wilfred's repentance and hand it to Wargrave.

The same principle would apply to Wargrave. Brown could seek his repentance while trying to stop him and bring him before lawful authorities. Compassion would not require leaving a dangerous man free to kill.

I would not solve the story with a convenient sacramental confession for Brown to reveal, either. His investigation must rely on observations and information people are free to share.

An imagined conversation with the judge could fail completely. Brown's offer of mercy might be especially unwelcome to someone who insists on being its dispenser, never its recipient.

## Conclusion — Could He Solve It?

![](father-brown-and-then-there-were-none-07.png)

My answer is a qualified yes. Father Brown could plausibly recognize the mind behind the island's murders and find a way to challenge its deceptions. The comparison with The Hammer of God gives that possibility real substance, rather than simply rewarding a favourite detective.

But identifying Wargrave, establishing what he did, and preventing further deaths would remain different tasks. Wargrave could improvise. Armstrong could refuse to talk. Brown himself could become a target.

In Christie's original ending, Vera shoots Lombard and then dies by suicide; Wargrave eventually kills himself too. His written confession leaves the police with an answer, but gives none of the victims another chance.

Brown's presence would offer another possibility, not a guaranteed happier conclusion. He might interrupt the plan because he refuses its basic invitation: to accept that understanding someone's guilt gives us complete possession of their life.

He would still need evidence, courage, and other people's help. The humility that makes him interesting is also what prevents this answer from becoming effortless.
