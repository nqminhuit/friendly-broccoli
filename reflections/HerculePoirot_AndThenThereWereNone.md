## Opening — An Extra Chair

![](poirot-and-then-there-were-none-01.png)

Imagine the boat pulling away from Soldier Island. The house waits above the rocks, the sea catches the afternoon light, and someone discovers that the dinner table needs another chair.

There are eleven visitors now. One of them has a beautifully tended moustache, immaculate clothes, and several reservations about the boat journey.

Hercule Poirot has arrived.

He is not in Agatha Christie's And Then There Were None. That absence helps make the novel so unsettling: nobody arrives with a familiar promise that the confusion will eventually become clear. Today, let us imagine giving the guests that unexpected companion. Would he understand them? Could he solve the mystery? And would understanding come soon enough to help?

## 1. Arrival — The Guest Nobody Planned For

![](poirot-and-then-there-were-none-02.png)

For our invented starting point, suppose Edward Armstrong consults Poirot about his peculiar invitation. The doctor has been summoned to attend the host's wife, but something about the arrangement troubles him. Poirot accompanies him unannounced. All ten original visitors still come.

This changes things immediately. The murderer has planned an elaborate sequence of deaths, but not a detective without a place in the accusations. An unexpected Poirot might make him postpone everything. Let us suppose his pride instead persuades him to begin the evening he has prepared.

Poirot would probably start with ordinary questions. Who has actually met the hosts? Who can confirm the reason for each invitation? Why do several accounts fail to fit together?

I can imagine him asking these things pleasantly, while his neighbours wonder whether this meticulous Belgian intends to make dinner difficult.

But we should not confuse a suspicious invitation with a solved murder. Poirot cannot inspect a face and discover a hidden history. He has no access to the private thoughts Christie shares with us.

Nor would being the extra guest make him automatically trustworthy. When the recorded accusations name everybody except him, someone might reasonably wonder whether he arranged the gathering.

That seems a useful first difficulty. Before Poirot can organize these people, he must persuade them that his questions are meant to help.

## 2. The Accusations — Guilt Is Not Evidence of This Murder

![](poirot-and-then-there-were-none-03.png)

The recording turns a polite evening into a public accusation. I imagine Poirot listening without interruption, then asking who supplied the information and how it could be checked. A confident voice is not a court.

Anthony Marston's death would destroy any remaining idea that this is merely a cruel joke. Cyanide works quickly. Poirot might identify the danger without being able to reverse what has happened.

In the original novel, Ethel Rogers has also been poisoned through her brandy. That happens before the company understands the pattern. Our detective's presence might bring her closer medical attention, but it cannot guarantee a rescue. For this imagined version, let us allow those first two deaths to occur.

By morning, Poirot has failed to save two people. His reputation is no comfort to them.

He would now need to distinguish two questions: what did these people do before arriving, and who is killing them here?

Someone may lie about an old crime while telling the truth about last night's movements. Someone else may confess honestly and still have nothing to do with the poison. Shame can explain secrecy without explaining the present murder.

[Pause:1.5s]

This is where Poirot's patience could matter. If every interview becomes a demand for confession, the guests will hide more. He needs their observations, including those they are embarrassed to explain.

## 3. The Guests — Different Ways of Living with Guilt

![](poirot-and-then-there-were-none-04.png)

The most interesting conversations would not necessarily be the ones that produce clues.

General Macarthur's withdrawal might concern Poirot more than a loud denial. The general has sent his wife's lover towards death and now seems ready to accept his own. Imagine Poirot joining him by the water, accepting a few moments of silence, then gently insisting they return together. A man who has stopped protecting himself is especially vulnerable here.

Emily Brent presents another problem. She believes her treatment of her pregnant servant was morally justified, despite the young woman's subsequent suicide. Poirot might find her certainty more disturbing than another person's tears. Yet cruelty in the past would not establish that she poisoned Marston.

William Blore offers professional confidence without dependable honesty. His false identity would need exposing, but his former police work should earn neither automatic trust nor automatic blame.

Philip Lombard would be a sharper opponent. He admits abandoning men to die in Africa, with a racial contempt Poirot should challenge, not mistake for admirable frankness. His readiness to acknowledge that act does not make his account of everything else reliable.

Vera Claythorne might move Poirot most. We know she engineered a child's drowning and tried to appear his brave rescuer. He would initially encounter an efficient young woman whose composure sometimes fails. I imagine him noticing when she stops listening to the conversation and follows a private memory instead. He could offer company without pretending that kindness excuses what she has done. Her distress invites careful questions, not an instant revelation of her secret.

And Thomas Rogers would need to be interviewed as a person, not left serving meals while the important people discuss murder.

The judge might seem the easiest ally: calm, experienced, and comfortable taking charge. Poirot would need to remember that being useful is not an alibi.

We readers know histories Poirot must still uncover. His advantage would be allowing different people different routes towards speaking. He might understand their guilt without accepting that anyone has acquired the right to execute them.

## 4. Fear — An Investigation That Changes the Story

![](poirot-and-then-there-were-none-05.png)

By now, our imagined events must diverge. We cannot add Poirot and then insist that everybody follows the original timetable.

After two deaths, I would expect him to press for shared precautions: stay together where possible, account for movements, supervise food and drink, and record medicines with witnesses. He should accept the same scrutiny as everyone else. Lombard's revolver would need an agreed, witnessed arrangement, not another private hiding place.

Signals to the mainland and a safe departure would remain priorities. Poirot is not entitled to keep people in danger because he would like to finish a puzzle.

None of this works by magic. Lombard may refuse cooperation. Blore may claim authority. Armstrong may resent questions about his medicines. Frightened people need sleep, and watching everybody becomes harder with every hour.

Still, even imperfect cooperation changes the killer's opportunities. Macarthur should not be left alone by the shore. Rogers should not be sent out alone to chop wood. Nobody's ordinary household task is more important than their safety.

I imagine Poirot also asking Armstrong to report any proposal for a secret investigation, however respectable the person proposing it.

That request might be decisive. The murderer relies not only on fear, but on private trust. Poirot's best defence would be making it possible to ask for help without feeling foolish.

## 5. The Solution — A Judge Can Also Be a Suspect

![](poirot-and-then-there-were-none-06.png)

Christie's original solution reveals why that matters. The murderer is Justice Wargrave. He recruits Armstrong into staging his apparent death, supposedly to help expose the killer. The doctor falsely confirms that the judge has been shot. Later, Wargrave kills his unwitting accomplice.

Armstrong has trusted the judge's standing when he should have questioned his purpose.

If that proposal arose in our version, imagine Armstrong knocking at Poirot's door. He has been asked to help with a secret plan. He begins by explaining that a man of Wargrave's position could surely be trusted.

In this scene of our own, I can hear Poirot answering: "His position, Doctor, is not the question. What has he asked you to do?"

Reporting the proposal would not itself prove murder. But Poirot could question its purpose and insist on witnesses, preventing Wargrave from controlling the arrangement alone.

Alternatively, if Poirot were deceived along with everyone else, his investigation could fail. He is not a doctor, and accepting medical testimony is not inherently foolish. His opportunity lies in questioning the circumstances and seeking independent confirmation, not possessing miraculous medical knowledge.

There is a further trap for us as readers. Wargrave's confession says Edward Seton, the man he sentenced, really was guilty. But Poirot cannot know that just because we have reached the final pages. Nor does a theatrical crime prove that the most judicial-looking suspect committed it.

He needs movements, access, contradictions, witnesses, and evidence connecting a person to the killings. He could reconstruct when the drinks stood unattended and compare who actually saw each movement with who merely assumed it. The private proposal would give him another specific account to test, not permission to stop investigating.

My answer is therefore yes: Poirot could plausibly identify Wargrave and break his scheme. A conviction would demand more work. Saving everyone is already impossible in the version we have imagined.

But I would not force Wargrave's original deception to survive all Poirot's precautions. A frustrated murderer might improvise, abandon the pattern, or attack the detective. Once we change the company, the ending becomes uncertain again.

## 6. Justice — Refusing the Murderer's Terms

![](poirot-and-then-there-were-none-07.png)

The deepest confrontation would be over what the island is for.

Wargrave describes both a desire to kill and a commitment to punishing the guilty. His confession arranges these impulses into an account he finds satisfying. Poirot would have to resist that satisfaction.

The guests have harmed people. Their victims matter. But the judge's information, his ranking of guilt, and his chosen punishments have never been independently tested. Calling the deaths justice does not remove his pleasure in arranging them.

Poirot himself is hardly free from pride or the temptation to manage other people's lives. That makes the encounter more interesting. To oppose Wargrave here, he would need to accept scrutiny and share responsibility, rather than simply replace one commanding voice with another.

[Pause:2s]

In the original ending, Vera kills Lombard and then hangs herself. Wargrave finally kills himself too; his written confession supplies the explanation. Knowing the answer brings nobody back.

For our alternate ending, I prefer a smaller image than a triumphant accusation: the remaining guests sharing one room until help can reach them. The case is unfinished, but nobody is being left alone to become the next verse.

That is a possible outcome, not a certainty. It would depend on cooperation, and it could still fail. Yet Poirot would not need to reconstruct every detail before taking measures that protect a life.

The first success might simply be getting everybody safely through another night.

## Closing — Who Gets to Leave?

![](closing_1.png)

Thank you for joining me in this imagined visit to Soldier Island.

I think Poirot would have a real chance—not because Christie owes her famous detective a victory, but because his questions could expose the trust and secrecy the plan requires. His presence would give the guests another person to turn to, though he would have to earn their confidence.

Would Armstrong tell him about a private proposal? Would Lombard cooperate? Could he persuade Vera to stay with the others when she most wanted to withdraw?

Those choices make this thought experiment interesting to me. The answer depends on more than one brilliant mind.

Which conversation do you think would change the story most? Please mark spoilers if you share your thoughts. And perhaps imagine that extra chair once more: not a guarantee of safety, but someone else who might notice when it becomes empty.
